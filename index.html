So what is cyber security?

It’s protecting your phone, laptop, accounts and data from people who want to steal or break them. You bank, study, shop and chat online every day, so this isn’t only an IT-department problem. It’s yours too.

Professionals split the field into areas: network, application, endpoint (your devices), cloud, information and operational security. You don’t need to memorise those. Four habits matter more than the labels:

  • Least privilege: nobody gets more access than their job needs.
  • Defense in depth: several layers, because any one layer can fail.
  • Zero trust: check first, trust later.
  • Separation of duties: no single person controls everything.

The CIA triad

Nearly every security rule serves one of three goals. Confidentiality: only the right people see the data (your WhatsApp chats are encrypted). Integrity: nobody changes it without permission (your bank balance shouldn’t move on its own). Availability: it works when you need it (a site shouldn’t fall over because of a DDoS attack).

What actually goes wrong

Malware

Malware is software built to harm you or steal from you. A virus hides in files and spreads when you open them. A worm spreads across networks by itself. A trojan pretends to be something useful. Ransomware locks your files and demands money. Spyware and keyloggers watch you and record what you type, passwords included. A botnet is a crowd of infected devices taking orders from one attacker.

Tricking people

Often it’s easier to fool a person than to break a computer. Phishing is a fake email or site that steals your details. Spear phishing targets one person, vishing happens over calls, smishing over SMS. Pretexting is a made-up story to get information, and baiting is leaving an infected pen drive lying around for someone curious.

Phishing checklist. Be suspicious if the message: acts urgent, comes from an odd address, asks for a password, OTP or card number, links to a different site than it claims, or is full of spelling mistakes.

Attacks on networks and websites

DDoS floods a site until it stops responding. Man-in-the-middle means someone quietly listens to a conversation. DNS spoofing sends you to a fake site. Session hijacking steals your logged-in session. Websites have their own list:

Password attacks

Brute force tries everything. Dictionary attacks try common words. Credential stuffing takes passwords leaked from one site and tries them elsewhere, which is the big reason you shouldn’t reuse passwords. Password spraying tries one popular password across many accounts.

Closer to home

Insiders cause breaches too, sometimes deliberately, often just by emailing a file to the wrong person. On phones, watch for fake apps, SIM swapping (a criminal gets a copy of your SIM) and juice jacking (booby-trapped public USB chargers). At the high end sit APTs (skilled groups that hide in a network for months), supply chain attacks and zero-days (bugs with no fix yet).

How to stay safe

If you do nothing else, do these:

  1. Use a different strong password for every account. A password manager makes this painless.
  2. Turn on two-factor authentication everywhere that offers it.
  3. Install updates. They fix the very bugs attackers use.
  4. Never give anyone your OTP or PIN. Nobody legitimate asks.
  5. Don’t tap unknown links. Install apps only from official stores and glance at the permissions.
  6. Take backups, and lock your devices.

Passwords

Length beats cleverness. password123 falls in seconds; something like BlueTrain-Coffee-Window-42 is long, easy to remember and hard to guess. Better still, let a manager generate random ones. Avoid personal details such as your name and birth year, because they’re the first things an attacker tries.

Logins can rest on something you know (password), something you have (phone, security key) or something you are (fingerprint, face). Combine two or more and you have MFA.

Handy tools

A firewall blocks unwanted traffic. Antivirus finds malware. A VPN encrypts your connection, but it won’t save you from phishing or a weak password. Disk encryption protects files if your laptop is stolen. And the 3-2-1 backup rule: three copies, on two kinds of storage, with one kept somewhere else.

Networks and cryptography

A firewall is the guard at the door. An IDS warns you about suspicious activity; an IPS also blocks it. Segmenting a network means one hacked corner doesn’t give away the whole building. For Wi-Fi, use WPA2 or WPA3 and change the router’s default password. Cloud providers follow a shared responsibility model: they secure the data centre, you secure your accounts and settings. Most cloud breaches are misconfigurations, like a storage bucket left public.

Cryptography in a few lines

Readable text is plaintext; scrambled text is ciphertext. Symmetric encryption (AES) uses one key to lock and unlock. Asymmetric (RSA, ECC) uses a public key to lock and a private key to unlock. Hashing (SHA-256) is one-way and can’t be reversed. The old Caesar cipher shifts each letter by a fixed number, so with a shift of 3, CAT becomes FDW. Trivial to break, but it shows the idea.

When your browser shows the padlock, it has checked the site’s certificate, agreed a secret session key with it, and now encrypts everything with that key.

Ethical hacking

Ethical hackers test systems with written permission to find weaknesses before criminals do. Without permission it’s illegal, full stop. The usual flow is reconnaissance, scanning, gaining access, checking whether access can be maintained, then a report on how to fix things. Testers work black box (no information), white box (everything, even source code) or grey box (some).

In exercises the red team attacks, the blue team defends and the purple team makes them cooperate. Common learning tools are Nmap, Wireshark, Burp Suite and Metasploit, and bug bounty programmes on HackerOne and Bugcrowd pay people who report flaws responsibly.

When something does go wrong

A SOC (Security Operations Center) watches systems around the clock using SIEM tools such as Splunk or Wazuh. Incident response runs: prepare, identify, contain, eradicate, recover, learn. Forensics keeps a chain of custody record of who handled evidence, so it holds up in court. Companies also keep disaster recovery plans for attacks, fires and power cuts.

Rules and frameworks

Risk is how likely a threat is to exploit a weakness, multiplied by how bad the damage would be. Common frameworks: NIST CSF, ISO 27001, CIS Controls and MITRE ATT&CK. Laws to know: India’s IT Act, 2000 and DPDP Act, 2023, CERT-In (India’s incident agency), the EU’s GDPR, the US HIPAA for health data, and PCI-DSS for card payments. Security and privacy aren’t the same thing: a company can store your data very securely and still misuse it.

Newer worries

Smart cameras and TVs often ship with weak default passwords and end up in botnets, so change them. Factories, railways and power plants run on control systems (SCADA) where an attack has physical effects. AI helps defenders spot odd behaviour, but it also helps attackers write convincing phishing and make deepfake voices and videos. Prompt injection means sneaking hidden instructions to a chatbot. Also watch for cryptojacking, QR code phishing and the dark web, which has legitimate uses but is also where stolen data gets sold.

Cyber safety in India

Scams you’ll actually meet

  • OTP fraud: a “bank” caller asks for your OTP.
  • UPI scam: you’re told to enter your PIN to “receive” money. You never need a PIN to receive money.
  • Fake job offers that ask for a registration fee.
  • KYC scam: “update KYC or your account will be blocked.”
  • Lottery scam: you “won”, but have to pay first.
  • Fake customer care numbers planted in search results.
If you’ve been scammed: call your bank immediately and block the card or account. Then call the cyber crime helpline 1930, report at cybercrime.gov.in, change your passwords and keep screenshots as proof.

If an account is hacked: disconnect the device, change passwords from a different safe device, turn on 2FA, scan for malware, and warn friends if your account was sending spam.

On social media: keep your profile private, don’t post your number, address or travel plans, ignore friend requests from strangers, and recheck privacy settings now and then.

Kids, parents and small businesses

Kids: share passwords only with your parents, don’t chat with strangers, keep your school name and photos off public posts, and tell a trusted adult if anything online feels wrong.

Parents: talk about what they do online, use parental controls and safe search, keep devices in shared rooms, and make it clear that saying no and asking for help is fine.

Small businesses: MFA on email, banking and accounting; backups you’ve actually tested; phishing training for staff; updated software and routers; access only where it’s needed.

Careers

Common roles: SOC analyst (watches alerts), penetration tester (legally hacks things), security engineer (builds defences), GRC analyst (risk and compliance), forensic analyst (investigates), and CISO (the head of security). Typical certificates go from Google Cybersecurity and CompTIA Security+, through CEH and CCNA, up to CISSP, CISA and OSCP. You don’t strictly need a degree, though many people have one.

A sensible path: networking and computer basics, then the Linux command line, then Python or Bash, then common attacks and defences, then lots of practice, then a certificate and a small portfolio. Practise legally on TryHackMe, Hack The Box or OverTheWire.

FAQ

Is a VPN enough? No. It protects your connection, not you from phishing, malware or weak passwords.

Are password managers safe? Good ones encrypt everything, and using one beats reusing passwords.

Do Macs and iPhones get viruses? Yes, less often, but they’re not immune. Keep them updated.
